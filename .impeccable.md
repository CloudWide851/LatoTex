## Design Context

### Users

LatoTex is for graduate students, researchers, and university faculty working primarily on Windows. They use it to move through a high-concentration manuscript workflow: LaTeX writing, citation checking, paper analysis, submission readiness, submission mail intake, and reviewer-response preparation. The interface should help them make the next correct decision without slowing expert work.

### Brand Personality

Trustworthy, calm, and professional. LatoTex should create confidence that manuscript state, credentials, diagnostics, and generated actions are precise and under the user's control. Its voice should be concise and direct, never playful, promotional, or alarmist.

### Aesthetic Direction

Use a restrained editorial-research-workbench direction with high information efficiency, quiet hierarchy, compact but readable controls, and clear state transitions. Preserve the existing light/dark themes, custom accent system, editor surface tokens, and desktop-first character. Avoid generic AI-dashboard aesthetics, decorative gradients/glow, nested card clutter, oversized marketing typography, and tutorial-heavy chrome that reduces expert density.

### Design Principles

1. Make the next safe action obvious: disabled, empty, error, and first-use states should explain the reason and provide one useful next step.
2. Keep expert density without sacrificing legibility: compact layouts remain acceptable, but labels, focus states, supporting text, and action targets must stay readable and operable.
3. Treat security as part of the interface: safe defaults, deterministic persistence, credential boundaries, and risky transport choices must be visible before an action runs.
4. Use the existing design system consistently: theme, accent, surface, border, status, typography, and reduced-motion behavior should come from shared tokens and primitives.
5. Prefer progressive disclosure over permanent complexity: show the common manuscript path first and reveal advanced configuration only when requested.
